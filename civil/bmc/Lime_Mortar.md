## 1. Mortar — Part 1: Introduction & Sand

### 1.1 Definition

**Mortar** is a paste prepared by adding the required quantity of water to a mixture of a binding material (cement or lime) and a fine aggregate (sand). The word is derived from the Latin *Moratorium*, meaning "crushed."

| Component | Role in Mortar |
|---|---|
| Binding material (cement/lime) | Called the **matrix**; provides strength, durability, quality |
| Fine aggregate (sand) | Called the **adulterant**; helps adjust strength |

---

### 1.2 Sand

Sand particles consist of small grains of **Silica (SiO₂)**, formed by the disintegration of sandstone.

#### Classification Based on Source

| Property | River Sand | Pit Sand | Sea Sand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source | River bed & banks | Pits 1–2 m below ground | Sea shores |
| Grain shape | Fine, rounded | Sharp, angular | Fine, rounded |
| Colour | Almost white | Orange-red | Light brown |
| Salts/impurities | Clean | Free from salts | Contains salts → attracts moisture |
| Main use | All engineering purposes (most widely used) | Mortar & concrete works | Basement filling only |
| Suitability | ✅ Highly suitable | ✅ Suitable | ❌ Not recommended for engineering works (causes efflorescence) |

#### Classification Based on Grain Size

| Type | Passes Through Sieve | Used For |
|---|---|---|
| Fine sand | 1.5875 mm | Plastering works |
| Coarse sand | 3.175 mm | Masonry works |
| Gravel sand | 7.62 mm | Concrete works |

#### Classification Based on Fineness Modulus (FM)
*(As per National Building Code)*

| Type | Fineness Modulus Range |
|---|---|
| Fine sand | 2.2 – 2.6 |
| Medium sand | 2.6 – 2.9 |
| Coarse sand | 2.9 – 3.2 |

> **Note:** For mortar preparation, sand FM should be **≥ 2.5 and ≤ 3.0** as per NBC.

---

### 1.3 Properties of Good Sand

- Chemically inert
- Grains should be clean, coarse, sharp, strong, angular, and durable
- Must NOT contain hygroscopic salts (e.g., CaCl₂, MgCl₂)
- Clay & silt content: max **3–4%** permitted for practical reasons
- No organic matter
- Should be **well graded**

---

### 1.4 Bulking of Sand

**Bulking of sand** is the increase in the volume of sand due to an increase in moisture content.

**Mechanism:**
- When water is added, a thin film forms around each sand particle.
- Surface tension causes particles to move apart → volume increases.
- **Finer the sand → greater the bulking.**
- Maximum bulking occurs at moisture content of about **5–8%.**

> **Impact on mix design:** Bulking causes an apparent increase in volume, which can lead to under-proportion of sand in a mix if volume batching is used. Always account for bulking.

---

### 1.5 Functions of Sand in Mortar

| Function | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Bulk** | Increases volume of mortar → reduces cost |
| **Setting** | When fat lime used, CO₂ is easily absorbed in sand voids → aids setting |
| **Shrinkage control** | Prevents excessive shrinkage and cracking after drying |
| **Strength** | Adjusts strength of mortar |
| **Surface area** | Subdivides paste into thin films → more surface area for spreading and adhering |

## 2. Mortar — Part 2: Classification

### 2.1 Overview of Classification

Mortar is classified based on:
1. Bulk density
2. Kind of binding material
3. Nature of application
4. Special mortars

---

### 2.2 Classification by Bulk Density

| Type | Bulk Density | Prepared From |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy weight mortar | ≥ 15 kN/m³ | Heavy quartz or other heavy sands |
| Light weight mortar | < 15 kN/m³ | Lightweight aggregates |

---

### 2.3 Classification by Binding Material

#### 1. Lime Mortar
- Made from fat lime or hydraulic lime + sand + water
- High plasticity → easy to place
- Good cohesion with surfaces; shrinks very little
- Sufficiently durable but **hardens slowly**
- Used for lightly loaded, above-ground parts of buildings
- **Consumption time:** 36 hours

| Sub-type | Characteristics | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Fat lime mortar | Shrinks greatly → requires 2–3× volume of sand; unsuitable for waterlogged/damp areas | Whitewashing, plastering; proportion lime:sand = 1:2 |
| Hydraulic lime mortar | Greater strength; suitable for damp areas | Underwater construction, masonry; proportion lime:sand = 1:2 |

---

#### 2. Surkhi Mortar
- Constituents: lime (binding material) + surkhi (fine aggregate) + water
- **Surkhi** = finely powdered burnt clay, free from admixtures and impurities
- Surkhi replaces sand either fully or partly in fat lime mortar
- Fineness: not more than **10% by weight** passing through fine sieve
- **Improves hydraulicity**
- Gives more strength than sand and is easily available

| ✅ Uses | ❌ Cannot be used for |
|---|---|
| Ordinary masonry (foundations & superstructure) | Plastering (disintegration occurs) |
| Construction of anicuts (dams) — historically | Pointing (disintegration occurs) |

> **Note:** Cement is NOT used as binding material in surkhi mortar due to chemical reaction issues.

---

#### 3. Cement Mortar
- Prepared by mixing cement + aggregate + water
- Proportion of cement to sand (by volume): **1:2 to 1:6** or more, depending on strength required
- Must be used **within 30 minutes** (before initial setting time of cement)
- **Consumption time:** 30 minutes
- Used where high strength is required: load-bearing walls, deep foundations, etc.

---

#### 4. Gauged Mortar (Composite / Lime-Cement Mortar)
- Lime mortar + cement added to improve quality and achieve early strength
- The process of adding cement to lime mortar is called **gauging**
- Binding material: cement + lime
- Usual proportion cement:lime (by volume) = **1:6 to 1:8**
- **Consumption time:** 2 minutes
- Used for bedding and construction of thick brick walls; economical

---

#### 5. Gypsum Mortar
- Prepared from gypsum (CaSO₄) as binding material
- Low durability in damp conditions
- Used in ancient Egyptian structures (Pyramids) and ornamental works

---

### 2.4 Classification by Nature of Application

| Category | Description |
|---|---|
| **Brick laying mortars** | For brickwork and walls |
| **Finishing mortars** | Mainly used for pointing and plastering work |

---

### 2.5 Special Mortars

| Type | Composition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Fire resistant mortar** | Aluminous cement : crushed fire brick powder = 1:2 | Fire brick lining of furnaces, fire places, ovens |
| **Light weight mortar** | Lime/cement mortar + sawdust / wood powder / cork / asbestos fibre / jute / coir | Sound-proof and heat-proof construction |
| **Packing mortar** | Cement-sand, cement-loam, or cement-sand-loam | Packing oil wells (watertight joints); high water resistance & strength |
| **Sound absorbing mortar** | Cement + lime + gypsum + slag (binders); pumice + cinders (adulterants) | Noise reduction in theatres and public halls; bulk density: **6–12 kN/m³** |
| **X-ray shielding mortar** | Heavy aggregate from heavy rock + suitable admixtures; elements: hydrogen, helium, cadmium | X-ray room plastering; bulk density **> 2200 kg/m³** |

## 3. Mortar — Part 3: Mix Proportions, Preparation & Testing

### 3.1 Recommended Mix Proportions by Nature of Work

| Nature of Work | Type of Mortar & Proportion |
|---|---|
| Pointing | Cement mortar 1:2 to 1:3 |
| Damp proof courses & cement concrete roads | Cement mortar 1:2 |
| General RCC work (lintels, pillars, slabs, stairs), reinforced brickwork, waterlogged/exposed areas | Cement mortar 1:3 |
| Partition walls & parapet walls | Cement mortar 1:3 |
| Plastering | Cement mortar 1:3 to 1:4; Lime mortar 1:2 |
| Internal walls & surfaces of less importance | Lime-cinder mortar 1:3 |
| Stone masonry with best quality stones | Lime mortar 1:2 |
| Stone masonry with ordinary stones, brickwork, foundations | Lime mortar 1:2 or Cement mortar 1:6 |
| Thin joints in brickwork | Lime mortar 1:3 or Fat lime |

---

### 3.2 Recommended Consistency of Mortar

| Application | Flow/Consistency (mm) |
|---|---|
| Laying perforated bricks | 70–80 mm |
| Laying solid bricks | 90–130 mm |
| Filling cavities | 130–150 mm |

---

### 3.3 Preparation of Lime Mortar

Two methods based on quantity:

#### 1. Pounding (Small quantity)
- Pits formed in hard ground, lined with bricks or stones on sides and bottom
- Pit dimensions: **~180 cm long × 40 cm wide (bottom) × 50 cm wide (top) × 50 cm deep**

#### 2. Grinding (Large quantity)
Two types of grinding mills:

| Mill Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Bullock driven grinding mill (Ghani)** | Circular trench: diameter 6–9 m, depth ~40 cm, width ~30 cm |
| **Power driven grinding mill** | Revolving pan: diameter 180–240 cm |

> **Most efficient method:** Power driven grinding mill

---

### 3.4 Tests for Mortar

#### 1. Crushing Test
- Brickwork is constructed using the mortar to be tested
- Sample is loaded in a **compression testing machine** until failure by crushing
- **Ultimate crushing strength = Maximum load ÷ Cross-sectional area**

#### 2. Tensile Strength Test
- Mortar placed in a briquette mould
- Central cross-sectional area of briquette: **38 mm × 38 mm**
- After curing, briquette is pulled in a **tensile testing machine**
- **Tensile strength = Ultimate load ÷ Central cross-sectional area**

#### 3. Adhesive Test
- Two bricks placed at right angles; mortar applied to form a horizontal joint
- For bricks of size 190 mm × 90 mm × 90 mm → joint area = **90 × 90 = 810 mm²**
- Upper brick suspended from overhead support; weights attached to lower brick
- Weights increased until separation occurs
- **Adhesive strength per mm² = Maximum load ÷ Joint area**

## 4. Lime — Part 1: Introduction, Key Terms & Constituents

### 4.1 Introduction

Lime is one of the **oldest binding materials**, used in ancient architectural works. It is a vital binding material in engineering construction, holding structural units together with sufficient strength.

**Uses:** Ordinary buildings, massive monuments, palaces, forts, etc.

**Sources of Lime (Lime is NOT available in free state):**
- Limestone hills
- Beds of old rivers
- Below ground level as **kankar**
- Shells of sea animals

**Disadvantages (Why lime is sometimes avoided):**
- Takes longer to harden compared to cement
- Lower strength compared to cement
- Cannot be stored for long durations

---

### 4.2 Properties of Lime

- Good plasticity
- Flexible and easily workable
- When used in mortar, provides greater strength to masonry
- Solidifies in less time and becomes hard
- Excellent binding properties — adheres perfectly to brick/stone masonry
- Highly resistant to moisture → suitable for pointing work

---

### 4.3 Key Terms

#### 1. Calcination
Heating limestone to redness in the presence of air at **800–900°C**.

**Chemical reaction:**
```
CaCO₃  →(800–900°C)→  CaO  +  CO₂
(Limestone)              (Quicklime)  (Carbon dioxide)
```

- **Pure limestone** (chalk) → gives **quicklime / caustic lime** (no impurities/clay)
- **Impure limestone** (kankar) → contains clay → hydraulic lime
- If **no impurities** → slaking is **fast** → called "quick lime"
- If **impurities present** → slaking is **slow**
- Quicklime obtained from kiln (practically) is called **lump lime**
- Heating carried out in clamps, intermittent kilns, or continuous kilns

---

#### 2. Slaking
Process of adding water to quicklime.

**Chemical reaction:**
```
CaO  +  H₂O  →(slaking)→  Ca(OH)₂  +  Heat
(Quicklime)                   (Slaked lime / Hydrated lime)
```

- **Exothermic reaction** (heat released)
- Product = **slaked lime / hydrated lime**
- Must be used immediately; otherwise absorbs CO₂ from atmosphere and reverts to limestone
- **Theoretical water requirement:** 32% of weight of CaO
- **Practical water requirement:** 2–3 times the amount of CaO
- Paste of slaked lime = **putty**
- Thin pourable suspension of slaked lime = **milk of lime**

**Relationship between slaking and setting:**

| Slaking | Setting |
|---|---|
| Fast slaking | Slow setting |
| Slow slaking | Fast setting |

---

#### 3. Setting
The process of hardening of lime after being converted into paste form.

---

#### 4. Hydraulicity
The property of lime to **set in the presence of water (dampness) and in the absence of air.**

- Less impurities (clay) → faster slaking → slower setting → lower hydraulicity
- **Clay is mainly responsible for hydraulicity in lime**
- Example: **Mullaperiyar Dam** used surkhi mortar (broken brick with high clay content → increased hydraulicity)

---

### 4.4 Binding Materials: Types Based on Setting Condition

| Type | Setting Condition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Air binding material** | Sets and gains strength only in presence of air | Gypsum, Acid resistant cement, Quick lime |
| **Hydraulic binding material** | Sets and gains strength in water (not necessarily air) | OPC, Hydraulic lime |
| **Autoclave binding material** | Sets only at high temperature and pressure | Lime-silica, Sand |

---

### 4.5 The Lime Cycle

```
CaCO₃ (Limestone)
    ↓ Calcination (heat, 800–900°C, loses CO₂)
CaO (Quicklime)
    ↓ Slaking (add H₂O)
Ca(OH)₂ (Slaked lime / Hydrated lime)
    ↓ Setting (absorbs CO₂ from air)
CaCO₃ (Limestone) — cycle complete
```

---

### 4.6 Constituents of Lime & Their Effects

| Constituent | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Clay** | Imparts hydraulicity; makes lime insoluble; ideal proportion: **8–30%** |
| **Magnesium carbonate** | Slakes and sets slowly (exception to norm); responsible for strength |
| **Sulphates** | Accelerates setting; reduces slaking |
| **Soluble silica** | Imparts hydraulicity at high temperature |
| **Alkalies & metallic oxides** | Should be up to **5%** |
| **Iron** | Should be present in small quantity only |
| **Pyrites** | **Harmful ingredient** — lime with pyrites must be rejected |

## 5. Lime — Part 2: Classification

### 5.1 Three Main Natural Types

#### 1. Fat Lime (Pure / High Calcium / Rich / White Lime)

- Calcium oxide (CaO) content: **≥ 95%**
- Clay content: **< 5%**
- Sets and hardens **only in presence of CO₂** (air)
- Slakes quickly; sets slowly in air; low hydraulicity
- Colour: **perfectly white**
- High degree of plasticity; soluble in water; hardens slowly
- **Uses:** Whitewashing, plastering walls, thick masonry walls, thin joints in brickwork

---

#### 2. Hydraulic Lime (Water Lime)

- Sets under water → hence called "water lime"
- Obtained from impure limestone (**kankar**)
- Increased clay content → more difficult to slake → increased hydraulic properties
- Useful in foundations and underwater work (sets in thick damp conditions with no air circulation)
- **Fat lime can be converted to hydraulic lime by addition of clay** (when suitable raw material unavailable)

**Sub-classification by clay content:**

| Type | Clay Content | Hydraulic Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Feebly hydraulic lime | Low clay | Weak hydraulic behaviour |
| Moderately hydraulic lime | Medium clay | Moderate hydraulic behaviour |
| Eminently hydraulic lime | High clay | Strong hydraulic behaviour |

---

#### 3. Poor Lime (Impure / Lean Lime)

- Very slow slaking and setting

---

### 5.2 BIS Classification of Lime for Building Purposes
*(As per IS 712: 1984)*

| Class | Name | Key Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Class A** | Eminently hydraulic lime | Structural purposes: arches, domes, etc. |
| **Class B** | Semi-hydraulic lime | General masonry |
| **Class C** | Fat lime | Finishing coat in plastering; whitewashing |
| **Class D** | Magnesium or dolomite lime | General work |
| **Class E** | Kankar lime | General work |
| **Class F** | Siliceous dolomitic lime | Undercoat and finishing coat of plastering |

## 6. Lime — Part 3: Manufacturing & Testing

### 6.1 Steps in Manufacturing Lime

1. Collection of raw materials
2. Calcination of limestone
3. Slaking of burnt lime

---

#### Step 1: Collection of Raw Materials

| Lime Type | Raw Material |
|---|---|
| Fat lime | Pure limestone |
| Hydraulic lime | Kankar (impure limestone) |

---

#### Step 2: Calcination of Limestone

Burning is carried out in **clamps** or **kilns**.

Kilns are of two types:
- **Intermittent kilns**
- **Continuous kilns**

Types of kilns used:

| Feature | Flame Kiln | Flare Kiln |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel arrangement | Alternate layers of limestone and fuel | Fuel ignited below an arch of limestone |
| Fuel contact with limestone | **Direct contact** | **No direct contact** |
| Arch | Not present | Rough arch of selected big limestone pieces formed first; smaller pieces packed over arch |
| Quality of lime | Standard | **Better quality** |
| Burning duration | ~3 days | — |
| Process | Kiln ignited from bottom; top covered with unburnt material; cooled and unloaded after 3 days | Fuel burned below arch; heat and flame rise through smaller limestone pieces above |

> **Twice-kilned lime:** Hard limestones are burned twice to obtain lime.

---

#### Step 3: Slaking of Lime

Slaking = adding water to quicklime.

**Slaking time:**

| Type | Slaking Time |
|---|---|
| Fat lime | 3–4 hours |
| Hydraulic lime | 12–48 hours |

**Two methods of slaking:**

**A) Slaking to Powder**
1. Quicklime broken into pieces of size **≤ 50 mm**
2. Immersed in water for a few seconds
3. Thrown on a wooden or masonry platform in a heap
4. Quicklime crumbles and falls into powder form
5. Alternatively, water can be sprinkled on a heap of quicklime → quicklime swells, crumbles, and falls as powder (this method used for lime from shells)

> **Important:** Over-burnt or under-burnt limestones do **not** slake easily. Such pieces must be removed before slaking.

**B) Slaking to Paste**
- Slaking completed in about **10 minutes**
- Product is in **paste form**

---

### 6.2 Tests for Lime

#### 1. Physical Properties Test

| Observation | Inference |
|---|---|
| White colour | Fat lime |
| Bluish grey / light brown colour | Hydraulic limestone |
| Clayey texture with earthy smell | Hydraulic lime |
| Presence of glistening particles | Presence of sand |

---

#### 2. Heat Test
- **Purpose:** Determine presence of calcium carbonate by analysing amount of CO₂ emitted
- **Procedure:**
  1. Weigh a piece of dry limestone
  2. Heat in open fire for a few hours
  3. Weigh again
  4. **Loss of weight = amount of CO₂ released** = indicator of CaCO₃ content

---

#### 3. Acid Test
- **Procedure:** Powdered lime dissolved in HCl, diluted, kept aside for 24 hours
- **Interpretation:**

| Observation | Inference |
|---|---|
| No residues | Pure lime |
| Residues/gel formation present | Impure lime / Hydraulic lime |
| Thick gel | **Class A lime** (eminently hydraulic) |
| Thin gel (can flow when tilted) | **Class B lime** (semi-hydraulic) |
| No gel formation | **Class C lime** (fat lime) |

---

#### 4. Ball Test
- 40 mm sized balls of lime paste prepared and kept for **6 hours**, then placed in water
- **Interpretation:**

| Observation | Inference |
|---|---|
| Expansion and disintegration of balls | **Class C lime** (fat lime) |
| No expansion | **Class B lime** (semi-hydraulic) |
