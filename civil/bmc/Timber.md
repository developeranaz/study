## 1. INTRODUCTION TO TIMBER

- The word **Timber** is derived from the old English word **"Timbrian"**.
- **Timber** = Wood used for engineering purposes.
- **Modulus of Elasticity of Timber:** 1 × 10⁴ N/mm² to 1.5 × 10⁴ N/mm²

---

## 2. PROPERTIES OF TIMBER

### 2.1 Weight
- Timber is **hygroscopic** in nature (absorbs moisture from the environment).
- Weight is determined at **12% moisture content**.
- **Specific gravity** = 1.54
- **Density** = 800–900 kg/m³

### 2.2 Strength
- Strength is determined both **along the grain** and **across the grain**.
- **Strength along grain** > **Strength across grain**
- Strength along grain is important for **columns and similar axially loaded members**.
- Strength across grain is important for **beams and railway sleepers**.
- Because strength along grain is higher, **sawing is always done parallel to the grain**.

### 2.3 Fire Resistance
Timber can be made fire-resistant by two methods:

**Method 1 – Sir Abell's Process (3 steps):**
1. Application of diluted sodium silicate.
2. Application of slaked lime.
3. Application of concentrated sodium silicate.

**Method 2 – Special Chemicals:**
- Borax solution
- Ammonium sulphate solution
- Sodium arsenite

### 2.4 Classification by Fire Resistance

| Type | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Refractory Timber** | Non-resinous; does not catch fire easily | Sal, Teak |
| **Non-Refractory Timber** | Resinous; catches fire easily | Chir, Deodar, Fir |

> **Note:** "Refractory" here means fire-resistant, NOT the same as the seasoning classification (see Section 3).

## 3. CLASSIFICATION OF TIMBER

### 3.1 Based on Position in Tree (IS 399-1963)

| Class | Description |
|---|---|
| **Standing Timber** | Timber contained in a living tree |
| **Rough Timber** | Timber obtained after felling a tree (before cutting) |
| **Converted Timber** | Timber sawn and cut into suitable commercial sizes |

### 3.2 Based on Modulus of Elasticity

| Group | Modulus of Elasticity (E) |
|---|---|
| **Group A** | E > 12.5 kN/mm² |
| **Group B** | E = 9.8 – 12.8 kN/mm² |
| **Group C** | E = 5.6 – 9.8 kN/mm² |

### 3.3 Based on Availability

|---|---|---|
| **X** | Most common | > 1,415 m³/year |

### 3.4 Based on Durability (Average Life in Service)

| Class | Average Life |
|---|---|
| **Highly Durable** | > 120 months (> 10 years) |
| **Moderately Durable** | 60 – 120 months (5–10 years) |

## 4. CLASSIFICATION OF TREES

Trees used in engineering are classified by their **mode of growth**:

### 4.1 Endogenous Trees
- Grow **inwards** (towards the centre).
- Show fibrous mass in longitudinal section.
- **Not used for engineering purposes.**
- **Examples:** Palm, Cane, Bamboo, Coconut.

### 4.2 Exogenous Trees
- Grow **outwards** — increase in bulk by forming **annual rings**.
- Age of tree = Number of annual rings.
- **Used for engineering purposes.**

Exogenous trees are further divided into:

| Feature | Conifers (Softwood) | Deciduous (Hardwood) |
|---|---|---|
| Leaf shape | Cone-shaped / needle leaves | Broad leaves |
| Colour & weight | Light colour, light weight | Dark colour, heavy |
| Nature | Resinous | Non-resinous |
| Strength | Weak | Strong |
| Annual rings | Distinct | Indistinct |
| Medullary rays | Indistinct | Distinct |
| Tensile strength | Strong in tension | Strong in compression, tension & shear |
| Grain strength | Strong along grain only | Strong along and across grains |
| Wood type | Yields softwood | Yields hardwood |
| Examples | Kai, Deodar, Chir, Fir, Pine | Teak, Oak, Sal, Mahogany |

> **Key Rule:** Maximum strength is along the grain (parallel to grain direction); Minimum strength is across the grain (perpendicular to grain direction).

## 5. STRUCTURE OF A TREE

Structure of wood is studied at two levels:

| Type | Observation Level |
|---|---|
| **Macrostructure** | Visible to the naked eye or at low magnification |
| **Microstructure** | Visible only at high/great magnification |

### 5.1 Macrostructure — Components (from centre to outside)

[Diagram: Cross-section of a tree trunk showing concentric zones: Pith → Heartwood → Sapwood → Cambium Layer → Inner Bark → Outer Bark; with radial Medullary Rays and Annual Rings visible]

1. **Pith / Medulla**
   - Innermost central core of the tree.
   - Provides nourishment at a young age; dies as tree ages.

2. **Heartwood**
   - Inner annual rings surrounding the pith.
   - **Dark in colour.**
   - Imparts **rigidity, strength and durability** to timber.
   - Does **not** take active part in tree growth (dead wood).
   - **Most valuable for engineering purposes.**

3. **Sapwood / Alburnum**
   - Outer annual rings between heartwood and cambium layer.
   - **Light in colour**, contains sap (nutrients and moisture).
   - Takes **active part in tree growth**; sap moves upward through it.
   - Weaker than heartwood.

4. **Cambium Layer**
   - Very thin layer of sap between sapwood and inner bark.
   - Contains sap that has not yet converted into sapwood.

5. **Inner Bark**
   - The inner skin/layer covering the cambium layer.
   - **Functions:** Protects the cambium layer from injury; supplies food to the trunk.

6. **Outer Bark (Cortex)**
   - Outermost protective layer.
   - Consists of wood fibre cells.
   - **Function:** Prevents moisture movement from the tree.

7. **Medullary Ray**
   - Thin radial fibres extending from the pith to the cambium layer.
   - **Functions:** Hold together the annual rings of heartwood and sapwood.
   - Responsible for **transverse (across-grain) strength** of timber.

8. **Annual Ring**
   - One ring formed per year of tree growth.
   - In Conifers: clearly visible.
   - In Deciduous: indistinct.
   - Used to calculate the **age of the tree** (count the rings).

### 5.2 Microstructure — Cell Types

| Cell Type | Function |
|---|---|
| **Conductive Cells** | Transmit nutrients from roots to branches and leaves |
| **Mechanical Cells** | Impart strength to the wood |
| **Storage Cells** | Store nutrients for living cells (found mainly in medullary rays, run horizontally) |

## 6. PROCESSING OF TIMBER

Processing of timber involves four main operations:
1. Felling of Trees
2. Seasoning
3. Conversion
4. Preservation

---

### 6.1 FELLING OF TREES

- **Definition:** Cutting down or knocking down trees and causing them to fall to the ground.
- **Age for felling:** 50 to 100 years (when trees have just matured or are near maturity).
- **Seasons to AVOID felling:**
  - **Autumn and Spring** — because sap is in vigorous motion during these seasons.
- **Proper Season for Felling:**
  - **Plain areas:** Mid-winter (water in sap evaporates during summer, giving drier wood).
  - **Hilly areas:** Mid-summer (heavy rainfall in winter makes operations difficult).

---

### 6.2 SEASONING OF TIMBER

**Definition:** The process of drying timber — i.e., removal of moisture (sap) from timber.

**Key Moisture Content Values:**

| Stage | Moisture Content |
|---|---|
| Newly felled tree | ~50% |
| After seasoning (optimum) | 8% – 12% (typically 10–12%) |

**Important Terms:**
- **Fibre Saturation Point:** The point at which cell cavities no longer contain free water (only bound moisture remains in cell walls).
- **Free Water / Free Moisture:** Moisture present in the cell cavities; major portion of total moisture in timber.
- **Bound Moisture:** Moisture present in the cell walls themselves.

**Objectives of Seasoning:**
1. To reduce weight of timber.
2. To increase strength and durability.
3. To improve dimensional stability (prevent warping, swelling, shrinking).
4. To protect from fungal attack.
5. To make timber more workable.

---

#### Methods of Seasoning

**A. Natural Seasoning / Air Seasoning**

- Very slow process; takes about **2 to 4 years**.
- Reduces moisture content to **10–20%** (specifically 10–15%).
- Timber stacked in the open with spacers between layers.
- **Spacer dimensions:** 35 mm × 25 mm to 50 mm × 35 mm (larger dimension = width).
- **Stack dimensions:**
  - Width of stack: restricted to ~150 cm.
  - Height of stack: restricted to ~300 cm.
  - Length of stack = Length of timber piece.
- Platform raised about **30 cm above ground level**.
- Minimum distance between adjacent stacks: **60 cm**.
- Results in **non-uniform seasoning**.
- **Advantages:** Cheaper; produces strong timber pieces.
- **Disadvantage:** Very slow; non-uniform.

**B. Artificial Seasoning Methods**

| Method | Description | Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Boiling** | Timber immersed in water and boiled | 3–4 hours | Quick but may reduce strength |
| **Chemical / Salt Seasoning** | Timber immersed in suitable salt solution (e.g. urea) and dried normally | Varies | Salt penetrates interior; interior dries faster than exterior |
| **Electrical Seasoning** | High-frequency AC current passed through timber | Rapid | Uniform; disadvantage: tendency to split |
| **Kiln Seasoning** | Timber placed in airtight kiln | Controlled | Most controlled method; controls circulating air, humidity & temperature |
| **Water Seasoning** | Timber immersed wholly in running stream water | 2–4 weeks | Sap replaced by water; dries easily; disadvantage: brittle timber; thicker/larger end faces upstream |

**Kiln Seasoning — Details:**
- Temperature required: **35–38°C**
- Types of kilns:
  - **Stationary Kiln / Compartment Kiln:** For small-scale seasoning; timber remains stationary.
  - **Progressive Kiln / Continuous Kiln:** Timber travels from one end to the other and gets seasoned progressively.

---

### 6.3 CONVERSION OF TIMBER (Sawing)

**Definition:** The process of cutting and sawing timber into usable sizes.

**Note:** During conversion, an allowance of **3 mm to 6 mm** must be made for shrinkage, squaring and planning.

#### Types of Sawing / Cutting Methods

**Strength Ranking (Highest to Lowest): R > Q > O > T**
**Mnemonic: RQOT** → Radial > Quarter > Ordinary > Tangential

| Method | How Cuts Are Made | Strength | Economics | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary / Flat / Bastard / Slab Sawing** | Cuts tangential to annual rings, parallel to each other | Low (3rd) | Most economical; minimum wastage | Warping and twisting |
| **Tangential / Plain / Flat-Grained Sawing** | Cuts tangential to annual rings, meeting each other at right angles | Lowest (4th) | — | Least strength |
| **Quarter Sawing** | Cuts made on each quarter; two successive cuts perpendicular to each other | Medium (2nd) | Less economical | Bends in transverse direction |
| **Radial / Rift Sawing** | Cuts parallel to medullary rays, used mainly in hardwood | Highest (1st) | Most uneconomical; more wastage | High wastage |

> - **Ordinary sawing** is the method **followed in India**.
> - **Radial sawing** is used for **decorative purposes**.

### 6.4 PRESERVATION OF TIMBER

**Definition:** Treatment of timber with chemicals or methods to increase its durability and protect it from decay, fungi, insects and weathering.

#### Properties of a Good Preservative:
1. Should be able to penetrate deeply into the wood.
2. Should be durable and effective for a long time.
3. Should not adversely affect the timber (no weakening).
4. Should not emit any offensive smell.
5. Should be fireproof.
6. Should be poisonous to fungi, white ants and other insects.
7. Should be cheap.

#### Common Preservatives

| Preservative | Composition / Process | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **AS-Cu Treatment (1:3:4)** | 1 part Arsenic Pentoxide + 3 parts Copper Sulphate (blue vitriol) + 4 parts Potassium/Sodium Dichromate; 6 parts AS-Cu mixed in 100 g water | Protects from white ants |
| **Chemical Salts** | Sodium fluoride, Mercury chloride, Zinc chloride, Copper sulphate | Odourless & non-flammable; timber immersed in solution |
| **Coal Tar (Tarring)** | Boiled coal tar applied on timber surface | Produces unpleasant smell; used for ground-embedded structures (posts) |
| **Creosote Oil (Bethel Full Cell Process)** | Obtained by distillation of tar; timber placed in airtight chamber; creosote injected at 50°C under 0.7–1 N/mm² pressure | Doubles life of timber; toxic; used in railway sleepers and piles; uneconomical |
| **Oil Paints** | 2–3 coats applied on surface | Preserves from moisture; increases durability |
| **Solignum Paint** | Toxic material applied as paint | Specifically resists termite (white ant) attack |

#### Methods of Applying Preservatives

| Method | Description | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Brushing** | Preservative applied to surface with brushes | Simplest method |
| **Charring** | Timber immersed in water for 30 min, then surface burnt to form a coal layer of **15 mm thickness** | No preservative used |
| **Dipping** | Timber dipped in preservative for a short period | — |
| **Steeping** | Timber soaked in preservative for a long period | Better penetration than dipping |
| **Hot and Cold Open-Tank Treatment** | Timber submerged in tank with preservative heated to **85–95°C** for a few hours, then allowed to cool | Provides protection to sapwood |
| **Injecting Under Pressure** | Preservative injected under pressure (typically used for creosote oil) | Most effective method; used where danger of fungi and insects is high |
| **Spraying** | Preservative solution sprayed using a spraying pistol under pressure | — |
| **Boucherie Process** | Pressure-based sap-replacement process | Fresh (green) timber used |

## 7. DEFECTS IN TIMBER

Defects in timber occur due to:
- Insects
- Conversion (sawing)
- Fungi
- Seasoning
- Natural forces

---

### 7.1 Defects Due to Insects

| Insect | Defect Caused |
|---|---|
| **Beetles** | Form pin holes of ~2 mm diameter; reduce wood to powder; rapid decay |
| **Marine Borers** | Found in saltwater (coastal areas); bore holes up to 25 mm diameter and 60 mm length |
| **Termites / White Ants** | Eat wood from core of the cross-section; very fast; live in colonies |

> **Note:** Teak and Sal naturally resist termite attack.

---

### 7.2 Defects Due to Conversion (Improper Sawing / Planing)

| Defect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Chip Mark** | Marks or signs on the surface due to improper planing or sawing |
| **Diagonal Grain** | Diagonal marks appearing on a straight-grained surface due to improper sawing |
| **Torn Grain** | Small depressions on the finished surface caused by a falling tool during planing |
| **Wane** | Presence of original rounded surface (bark side) on a manufactured piece of timber |

---

### 7.3 Defects Due to Fungi

| Defect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Blue Stain** | Sap of wood stained bluish by fungi action |
| **Sap Stain** | Sapwood loses colour because fungi feeds on cell contents |
| **Wet Rot** | Fungi causes chemical decomposition; wood converts to greyish-brown powder; due to alternate wetting and drying; reddish-brown powder formed |
| **Dry Rot** | Fungi attacks wood in absence of air/poor ventilation; wood converts to dry powder form |
| **White Rot** | Fungi attacks **lignin** of wood; wood changes to white colour |
| **Brown Rot** | Fungi attacks **cellulose** of wood; wood changes to brown colour |
| **Heart Rot** | Branch cut from tree exposes heartwood to atmosphere; heartwood affected, becomes hollow, weakening the tree |

> **Memory Aid for Rots:**
> - White Rot → attacks **L**ignin → **L**ight colour (white)
> - Brown Rot → attacks **C**ellulose → Colour changes to brown

---

### 7.4 Defects Due to Seasoning

| Defect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Bow** | Curvature formed in the **direction of length** of timber |
| **Cup** | Curvature formed in the **transverse direction** of timber |
| **Twist** | Timber spirally distorted along its length |
| **Warp** | Timber twisted out of shape (general term for any distortion out of plane) |
| **Check** | A crack that separates wood fibres but does **not** extend from one end to the other |
| **Split** | A crack (check) that extends from **one end to the other** |
| **Collapse** | Uneven shrinkage causes wood to flatten during drying |
| **Honeycombing** | Stresses during drying cause radial and circular cracks in the **interior** of timber |
| **Case Hardening** | Exposed outer surface dries rapidly while interior is still wet; outer surface is in **compression**, inner surface is in **tension** |
| **Radial Shake** | Cracks follow the direction of annual rings and medullary rays |

---

### 7.5 Defects Due to Natural Forces

| Defect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Burls / Excrescence** | Irregular projections on the tree body due to shock/injury at a young age |
| **Callus** | Soft tissue/skin that covers the wound of a tree |
| **Chemical Stain** | Discolouration due to chemical action from external agency |
| **Coarse Grain** | Annual rings widened due to rapid growth; timber possesses **less strength** |
| **Dead Wood** | Timber obtained from a dead standing tree |
| **Druxiness** | White decayed spots concealed by healthy-looking wood (hidden decay) |
| **Foxiness** | Red or yellow tinge in wood around the pith; caused by poor ventilation during storage, over-maturity, or growth in marshy soil |
| **Knot** | Dark, hard rings at the point where a branch was removed/broken/cut |
| **Rind Galls** | Peculiar curved swellings found on the body of the tree |
| **Twisted Fibres / Wandering Hearts** | Twisting of young trees due to fast-blowing wind |
| **Upsets / Ruptures** | Wood fibres injured by crushing or compression |
| **Water Stain** | Discolouration when wood comes into contact with water |
| **Wind Cracks** | Exterior surface shrinks due to atmospheric exposure, resulting in surface cracks |

#### Classification of Knots

**By Size (Diameter):**

| Type | Diameter |
|---|---|
| **Pin Knot** | Up to 6.50 mm |
| **Small Knot** | 6.50 mm – 20 mm |
| **Medium Knot** | 20 mm – 40 mm |
| **Large Knot** | > 40 mm |

**By Form and Quality:**

| Type | Description | Engineering Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Dead Knot** | Fibres NOT interconnected with surrounding wood; easily separated | Not used |
| **Live Knot** | Sound, free from decay and cracks; cannot be separated from body | Can be used |
| **Decayed Knot** | Unsound; formed by fungi action | Not used |
| **Loose Knot** | Preliminary stage of dead knot | Not used |
| **Tight Knot** | Preliminary stage of live knot | Can be used |
| **Round Knot** | Cross-section is round or oval | Depends on quality |

#### Types of Shakes

Shakes are cracks that **partly or completely separate** the fibres of wood.

| Type | Description | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Cup Shake** | Curved crack separating one annual ring from another | Circular direction (along the ring) |
| **Ring Shake** | Cup shake that covers the **entire ring** | Circular (full ring) |
| **Heart Shake** | Crack occurs at the **centre (pith)**, extends outward towards sapwood/bark in direction of medullary ray | Centre → Outside (pith to bark) |
| **Star Shake** | Cracks extend **from bark towards sapwood/pith**; wider outside, narrower towards pith | Outside → Inside |
| **Radial Shake** | Similar to star shake; crack extends from bark to pith, passing through annual rings | Bark → Pith (through rings) |

> **Exam Tip:** "From bark to pith" = **Radial Shake** *(not Star Shake — Star shakes only go into the sapwood, not all the way to pith)*

## 8. MARKET FORMS OF TIMBER

|---|---|---|---|

## 9. INDUSTRIAL FORMS OF TIMBER

Timber scientifically prepared in a factory. Major types:

### 9.1 Veneers (IS 303-1989)
- **Definition:** Thin sheets or slices of wood of superior quality.
- **Thickness:** 0.40 mm to 6 mm (or more).
- **Suitable timbers:** Mahogany, Oak, Rosewood, Sissoo, Teak.
- **Veneering:** The process of preparing veneer sheets.
- **Uses:** Used to produce plywood, batten boards, lamin boards; glued onto inferior timber to improve appearance.

### 9.2 Plywood
- **Definition:** Boards prepared from thin layers (plies) of veneers glued together.
- **Ply:** Thin layer.
- Veneers arranged in **odd numbers (3 to 13 plies)** placed **perpendicular to each other** (cross-bonded).
- Specified by **number of plies** or **thickness**.
- **Uses:** Doors, furniture, partitions, railway coaches, wall panelling.

**Types of Plywood Core Boards:**

| Type | Core Description | Core Width |
|---|---|---|
| **Batten Board** | Solid core; large pieces | Core up to 80 mm wide |
| **Lamin Board** | Core strips | Each strip ≤ 7 mm thick |
| **Block Board** | Core strips; used in bus bodies, railway carriages | Core strips ≤ 25 mm wide |
| **Multi-Ply** | More than three plies | — |

### 9.3 Fibreboards / Pressed Wood / Reconstructed Wood
- **Definition:** Rigid boards made from compressed wood fibres.
- **Thickness:** 3 mm to 12 mm.
- **Cannot take structural loads.**
- **Uses:** Interior decoration, doors, partitions.

**Types of Fibreboards:**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Hardboard** | Heavily pressed wood; strong, durable; least affected by temperature |
| **Laminated Board** | Light and strong; used in walls and ceilings |

### 9.4 Impreg Timber
- **Definition:** Timber fully or partly immersed in resin (synthetic resin penetrates the wood cells).
- **Resin used:** Phenol Formaldehyde.
- **Curing temperature:** 150°C – 160°C.

### 9.5 Compreg Timber
- Same process as Impreg timber, **except** curing is carried out **under pressure**.
- **Strength and durability** of Compreg > Impreg.
- **Specific gravity:** 1.3 – 1.35.
